# What Is the Vastu Purusha Mandala: The 81-Pada Plan

## Introduction

Anyone who wants to understand Vastu Shastra seriously has to start with the Vastu Purusha Mandala. It is a cosmic diagram, and Indian tradition treats it as the foundational plan for temples, homes and towns. Vastu tradition holds that when a house or building is laid out in step with this diagram, the people who live in it find an atmosphere of harmony, comfort and spiritual growth.

## What Is the Vastu Purusha Mandala?

The Vastu Purusha Mandala is a sacred geometric grid. It is often drawn as a square diagram in which a cosmic being, the Purusha, is pinned down by 45 deities. The diagram divides space into zones, and each zone is linked with the energy of a particular deity. In Vedic tradition it is more than a symbol. It is also a practical tool of architecture, and temples, homes and towns have been planned on its mathematics and philosophy.

The most common grid has 81 squares, a 9x9 arrangement called the Paramasayika Mandala. Its central part is the Brahmasthana, dedicated to Brahma, the creator. The other 44 deities stand around it, and each is believed to govern a different side of life and nature. In this way the energies are thought to move in balance through the whole building.

## Key Aspects of the Vastu Purusha Mandala

### Cosmic Diagram: The Reclining Deity and the 45 Energy Fields

- The Vastu Purusha is shown lying diagonally across the grid, with his head in the northeast (Ishana) and his feet in the southwest (Nairitya).
- The diagram is divided by horizontal, vertical and diagonal lines. These lines are likened to the veins through which energy flows.
- There are 45 deities in all: Brahma at the centre, 12 in the inner ring and 32 on the outer perimeter. Together they form a map of energy fields, each linked with a different part of life.

### Sacred Geometry: The 9x9 Grid

- The Paramasayika Mandala, which divides the space into 81 squares (padas), is the one most often used for homes and palaces.
- Each pada is believed to have its own spatial and energetic quality. This is why rooms such as the kitchen, bedroom or prayer room are placed according to the character of the deity of that zone.
- The grid gives mathematical precision, and through it the built space is meant to be brought into step with the rhythms of nature.

### Foundational Blueprint: Planning a Building by Vastu

- From the position of walls and doors to the direction of rooms, the Vastu Purusha Mandala is treated as the template for every part of a building.
- The aim is that the building stays in harmony with the five elements: fire, air, water, earth and space.
- Each kind of building has its own division: 81 squares for houses, 100 for temples and so on. This shows how flexible the system is within Vastu Shastra.

## How the Grid Is Laid Out

Applying the mandala begins with Pada Vinyasa, in which the plot is divided into equal squares (padas). A 9x9 grid is used for a home. The central nine squares are kept for Brahma, and the other 44 deities take their fixed places around them. The plot is taken to lie so that the head of the Purusha points towards the northeast.

The basic steps are these:

- Draw a square and divide it into 9 equal parts along both axes. This makes a grid of 81 squares.
- Mark the centre as the Brahmasthana, the seat of Brahma.
- Using the traditional charts, assign each square to its deity.
- Decide the function and position of each room according to the nature of these energy fields.

## How the Grid Is Read

Vastu practitioners look at which deity's energy is assigned to each square and which room suits it. For example:

- The northeast is regarded as highly auspicious. By tradition a prayer or meditation room is placed here, because the direction is linked with clarity and the flow of cosmic energy.
- The central Brahmasthana should be kept open and uncluttered, because it is the heart of the home.
- The zones of deities associated with wealth, prosperity or protection are considered suitable for main rooms such as the living room, or for a strongroom.

Vastu tradition holds that correct placement supports health, prosperity and peace, while poor placement can bring obstacles. This is a traditional belief and not a promise of any result.

## Practical Applications

### Residential Construction

Before a new house is built, the architect uses the Vastu Purusha Mandala to decide the right place for every room, for the entrance, and for smaller things such as windows and the prayer space. Tradition holds that putting each function in the energy field that suits it brings more happiness into the home.

### Temple and Town Planning

In larger projects, such as temples or towns, grids of different sizes are used (for example 100 squares for temples). Each zone is set aside for a particular use, such as the sanctum, administrative areas or the marketplace. The aim is that the built space matches its spiritual purpose and its social function.

## Common Misconceptions

- **It is only for temples:** Many people think the Vastu Purusha Mandala applies only to temples or palaces. In fact it is the basis of every kind of building, from homes to towns.
- **There is only one grid:** Several grid sizes are recognised, from 1x1 up to 32x32. A different grid is thought suitable for each type of construction, not only the 9x9.
- **It is only a symbol and has no practical use:** It is deeply symbolic, but its use in design is also practical and mathematical. It helps in dividing a space sensibly.

## Examples

- **A modern home:** In a house planned on the 9x9 grid, the living room is placed in the north, whose ruler is held to be the lord of wealth (Kubera). The kitchen is placed in the southeast (Agneya), the direction of fire. Tradition regards this arrangement as favourable for the home.
- **Jaipur:** The city of Jaipur is often cited as an example of a town planned on Vastu principles, with a layout that closely resembles the Vastu Purusha Mandala. It is also described as a planned city of long standing.

## Conclusion

Understanding the Vastu Purusha Mandala is the key to bringing the principles of Vastu Shastra into everyday life and construction. As a cosmic diagram it shows the harmony of the physical and the spiritual. Whether the project is a home or a temple, Vastu tradition holds that building in step with this mandala keeps the structure in tune with cosmic energies.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many deities and how many padas does the Vastu Purusha Mandala have?

The most common mandala has 81 padas (9x9) and 45 deities. Brahma is at the centre, 12 deities stand in the inner ring and 32 on the outer perimeter.

### In which directions are the head and feet of the Vastu Purusha placed?

The Vastu Purusha is taken to lie diagonally across the grid. His head is in the northeast (Ishana) and his feet are in the southwest (Nairitya).

### Why is the Brahmasthana kept empty?

The Brahmasthana is the centre of the grid and is dedicated to Brahma. It is regarded as the heart of the home, so tradition says it should be kept open and free of heavy objects.

### Is this mandala only for houses?

No. A grid of 81 squares is used for houses, 100 squares for temples, and similar grids are used in town planning. The recognised grids range from 1x1 to 32x32.

### How can a beginner start to understand it?

First learn the layout of the 9x9 grid, then learn the deity of each square and the area it covers. After that, practise by looking at the rooms of your own home against these zones. Reading introductory guides and speaking to an experienced Vastu consultant also helps.
