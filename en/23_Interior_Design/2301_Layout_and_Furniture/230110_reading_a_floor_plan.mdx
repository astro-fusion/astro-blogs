# How to read a floor plan before you buy or rent

A floor plan is a drawing of the flat or house as if the roof were lifted off. After reading this you will be able to find the scale, work out real room sizes, understand the area terms, spot the details that decide whether your furniture fits, and know what to ask on a site visit.

## Step 1: find the scale

Every plan is drawn smaller than real life. The scale tells you how much.

- **A ratio such as 1:100.** One unit on paper equals 100 units in the building. So 1 cm on paper is 100 cm, which is 1 m.
- **1:50** is more detailed: 1 cm on paper is 50 cm in the building.
- **A scale bar** is a short ruler printed on the drawing. Use it when the plan has been photographed, enlarged or reduced, because a ratio is only right at the original print size.
- **No scale?** Look for written dimensions and use those. Do not guess by eye.

Dimensions are often written in millimetres (3200 means 3.2 m) or in feet and inches. If the plan shows both, check they agree.

## Step 2: read the walls and openings

- **Thick lines are walls.** An outer brick wall is often about 23 cm (9 in) thick, and an inner partition about 11.5 cm (4.5 in). These are common thicknesses, not rules. Your plan may differ.
- **Solid black or hatched squares** are usually columns. Columns hold up the building. Do not plan on removing one, and ask a licensed engineer before touching any wall or column.
- **A gap in a wall with an arc** is a door. The arc shows how far the door swings. Common widths are 90 cm (2 ft 11 in) for a main door, 80 cm (2 ft 7 in) for rooms and 75 cm (2 ft 6 in) for a bathroom. Furniture inside that arc will block the door.
- **Thin double or triple lines** in a wall are windows. Look for the sill height if it is written. A low sill leaves less wall for a wardrobe or bed.
- **Sliding doors** are drawn as overlapping lines, with no arc.
- **Dashed lines** often show things above you, such as a beam or loft.
- **A north arrow** shows which way the plan faces. Use it to see which rooms get morning sun and which get the afternoon heat.

## Step 3: understand the area terms

Floor areas in sale brochures are not all the same measure.

- **Carpet area:** the floor you can actually carpet, inside the walls. It excludes wall thickness. This is the figure most closely tied to your furniture.
- **Built-up area:** carpet area plus the thickness of walls, and often balconies and terraces.
- **Super built-up area:** built-up area plus a share of common space such as lobbies, stairs and lifts.

Builders in different places use these differently. Ask for the carpet area in writing for each room, and compare flats on carpet area, not on the headline figure.

## Step 4: measure rooms from the plan

Take a ruler and measure the room's inside width and length on the paper. Multiply by the scale.

### Worked example: a bedroom on a 1:100 plan

On the plan a bedroom measures 3.2 cm by 4.0 cm. The scale is 1:100.

**Step 1: convert to real size.** 3.2 cm on paper is 3.2 x 100 = 320 cm, which is 3.2 m (10 ft 6 in). 4.0 cm is 400 cm, which is 4.0 m (13 ft 1 in).

**Step 2: find the area.** 3.2 x 4.0 = 12.8 square metres. One square metre is about 10.76 sq ft, so 12.8 x 10.76 is about 138 sq ft.

**Step 3: allow for finishes.** Plaster and tiles can take 1 to 2 cm (under 1 in) off each wall. So the usable room may be a little smaller than the drawing.

**Step 4: test the bed.** Put a double bed (150 x 190 cm) with its headboard on the 3.2 m wall. Width: 320 - 150 = 170 cm, so 170 / 2 = 85 cm (2 ft 9 in) each side if centred. That is above the 75 cm (2 ft 6 in) aim.

**Step 5: test the length.** The bed and headboard take 190 + 5 = 195 cm (6 ft 5 in). The room is 400 cm, so 400 - 195 = 205 cm (6 ft 9 in) is left.

**Step 6: test the wardrobe.** A wardrobe 60 cm (2 ft) deep at the foot wall leaves 205 - 60 = 145 cm (4 ft 9 in) of walking space. Comfortable.

**Step 7: check the door.** If the door is 80 cm (2 ft 7 in) wide, draw its swing. If it lands on the bed or wardrobe, the room will be tight even though the numbers add up.

The room fits. If the numbers had come out below 60 cm (2 ft) on a side, you would choose a smaller bed or a different wall.

## Step 5: look at the whole flat, not just the rooms

- **Circulation:** how many steps from the front door to the kitchen, bathroom and bedrooms? Long passages waste area.
- **Kitchen:** see if the fridge, sink and hob can be in order, and where the window is for light and smoke.
- **Bathrooms:** check for a window or exhaust point, and where the wet area is.
- **Balcony and utility:** is there a place for washing, drying and a water filter?
- **Light and air:** two openings on different walls let air cross. A room with one window gets less movement of air in the plains in summer.
- **Neighbours' walls:** a bedroom shared with a lift shaft or a main road is louder than one on the quiet side.
- **Storage:** where does a wardrobe go, and is there a shoe area at the door?
- **Shared and joint family use:** will the grandparents' room be near a bathroom and away from steps?

## Checklist with a tape measure

1. Find the scale and confirm it with one written dimension.
2. Measure each room on paper and convert it.
3. Compare carpet area with the figure in the agreement.
4. Draw your bed, wardrobe and sofa to scale on tracing paper and slide them around the plan.
5. Mark each door swing.
6. At the site visit, measure the same rooms with a tape and compare.
7. Check the north direction and window positions at the time of day you use the room.
8. Ask what is not shown: beams, ducts, pipes, ledges and lowered ceilings.

## Common mistakes

- **Trusting the headline area** without asking for carpet area.
- **Ignoring the door swing,** which keeps more than half a square metre of floor clear and unusable.
- **Judging by a photo of the plan,** which may have been reduced or enlarged.
- **Forgetting wall finishes,** so a tight room becomes tighter.
- **Not visiting at a different time of day** to see light and noise.

## FAQ

### What does 1:100 mean?

One unit on paper stands for 100 of the same unit in real life, so 1 cm is 1 m.

### What is the difference between carpet and super built-up area?

Carpet area is the floor inside your walls. Super built-up adds walls, balconies and a share of common space.

### How do I know which wall is a column?

Columns usually show as solid or hatched squares or rectangles. Ask the builder or an engineer to confirm.

### Can I change the layout after buying?

Moving light partitions may be possible, but anything that touches columns, beams or plumbing stacks needs a licensed engineer and permission from the owner or society.

### How do I check that my furniture will fit?

Convert the room to real size, then mark the furniture on the plan or on the floor with tape. Leave the clearances you want, such as 75 cm (2 ft 6 in) beside a bed.
