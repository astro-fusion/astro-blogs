# A handover checklist for a new flat

The day you take the keys is the day you have the most say. After reading this you will be able to inspect a new flat room by room, write down every fault in a snag list, compare the rooms with the plan, and ask for repairs before you move in.

This is a practical checklist, not legal advice. For anything about the sale agreement, ownership papers or payments, check local rules and talk to a lawyer. For anything structural, electrical, gas or waterproofing, ask for a licensed engineer or electrician.

## Before you go

- Take the plan and the agreement, and read what was promised: room sizes, fittings, flooring, fixtures and any extras.
- Bring a tape measure, a torch, a phone with a charger, a small spirit level or a ball bearing, a plug-in lamp or phone charger, a pencil, tape and a notepad. A friend helps: one person tests while the other writes.
- Go in daylight on a dry day if you can, and go again in the evening to see the lights. If it rained recently, check ceilings and corners for marks.
- Ask for the person who handles the handover, and ask them to walk you through. Do not rush. Allow a few hours.

## Walk through in this order

### The door and the common areas

Check the main door opens and shuts smoothly, the lock works with every key, and you are given all the keys. Count them and write the number down. Look at the stair, the lift, the water tank and the meter room, and ask who looks after each.

### Walls, ceilings and floors

Look along every wall with your torch held flat against it, since a low beam shows bumps and cracks. Hairline cracks in plaster are common, but wide, long or stepped cracks need a licensed engineer to look at them. Look for damp patches, stains and bubbling paint, especially near the bathroom and on top-floor ceilings.

Tap tiles gently with a coin or knuckle: a hollow sound can mean a tile is loose. Check the floor for chips and uneven joints. Roll a ball or use a level to see whether the floor slopes toward the drain in wet areas.

### Doors and windows

Open and close each door and window. Check they close fully, latches work, glass is unbroken, and there is no gap you can see daylight through. Check the mosquito mesh if one is fitted. Look at the sills for water marks. Measure a door that you will have to move large furniture through.

### Electrical points

Do not open anything. Just test what you can see. Plug a lamp or charger into every socket and flip every switch, one after another. Note any socket that is dead, loose or warm, and any switch that does not do what it should. Look for the position of the distribution board and ask that it be labelled. Any problem goes to a licensed electrician.

### Kitchen

Run every tap, hot and cold if fitted, and look under the sink for drips. Check the drain empties fast. Look at the counter and cabinets for chips, and open every door and drawer. Ask where the gas arrangement, if any, will be installed and by whom. Check the exhaust or chimney point.

### Bathrooms

This is where most faults hide.

- Run the taps and the shower for a few minutes.
- Check the water flows to the drain and does not pool.
- Flush the toilet several times.
- Look under basins and around pipes for leaks.
- Check the floor slope again with a ball.
- Look at the grout and edges for gaps.
- Ask how the floor was waterproofed and if you have any record or warranty in writing.

### Balcony and terrace

Check the railing is firm and the height feels safe. Look at the floor slope toward the drain. Do not load a balcony with heavy planters or equipment without asking an engineer.

### Water, power and records

Note the electricity meter reading, the water meter reading if there is one and the date, and photograph them. Ask who provides the water, when, and how much the tank holds, and ask where the water pump or motor is, if one exists.

## Worked example: does the room match the plan?

The plan shows a bedroom of 3.0 x 3.6 m (10 x 12 ft). You measure the finished room and get 2.95 x 3.57 m (9 ft 8 in x 11 ft 8 in).

1. Plan area: 3.0 x 3.6 = 10.8 square metres.
2. Measured area: 2.95 x 3.57 = 10.53 square metres.
3. Difference: 10.8 - 10.53 = 0.27 square metres.
4. Differences of a few centimetres in each direction are normal, because plaster, tiles and the rounding on plans all add or take away a little. The plan may also quote the room to the middle of the wall.
5. If a room is short by much more than this, or the flat as a whole is clearly smaller than what you agreed to buy, note it on the snag list and ask the builder to explain, in writing. Check your agreement for how areas are measured and what difference is accepted.

Always measure the places where you will put a bed, a wardrobe or the kitchen units, since a few centimetres decide whether a wardrobe door opens.

## Write a snag list

For each fault, write the room, the problem, a photo number and a date. Use a simple table.

| Room | Fault | Photo | Fixed? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bedroom 1 | Crack above the window | 12 | |
| Bathroom 2 | Tap drips | 21 | |

Hand the builder a copy. Ask them to sign and date it, and to say when each item will be fixed. Keep your own copy, and check again after the repairs, since a repair can create a new mark.

Many people prefer to sign for the keys only after the list is agreed. Ask your lawyer what is right for your agreement.

## Checklist

- Plan, agreement and a list of promised fittings in hand.
- Tape measure, torch, charger, notepad, spirit level or ball.
- Keys counted and every lock tested.
- Walls, ceilings, floors and tiles checked for cracks, damp and hollow sounds.
- Doors and windows opened, closed and checked for gaps.
- Every switch and socket tested with a plug-in lamp.
- Every tap, shower, toilet and drain run and checked for leaks.
- Meter readings photographed with the date.
- Water arrangement, tank, pump and lift explained.
- Snag list written, photographed, signed and dated by both sides.
- Papers for warranties, plans and fittings collected.

## Common mistakes

- **Rushing.** A quick look at noon in a rush misses cracks, leaks and slopes.
- **Skipping the bathrooms.** Leaks here are costly to fix once furniture and tiles are in.
- **Testing nothing.** Plug in and flip every switch yourself.
- **Taking promises by word of mouth.** Put each promised repair in writing with a date.
- **Moving furniture in before the snag list is settled.** It becomes harder to prove what was damaged and when.

## FAQ

### How long should a handover inspection take?

Allow a few hours for a flat of 500 to 1,200 sq ft, and more if you want to wait for water to run in the bathrooms. Do not let anyone hurry you.

### What if the room sizes differ from the plan?

Measure carefully and note the difference on the snag list. Small gaps are normal. For larger gaps, ask the builder to explain in writing and check your agreement and local rules.

### Should I bring a professional?

For structural worries such as wide cracks, ask a licensed engineer. For wiring concerns, ask a licensed electrician. Some people also bring a friend who has done it before.

### Who fixes problems after I move in?

That depends on your agreement and local rules. Ask before you accept the keys what the builder is responsible for and for how long, and get it in writing.

### Can I test the water supply?

Yes. Run taps in the kitchen and bathrooms, flush toilets and check the pressure. Ask about the tank and pump, and check again at different times of day.
