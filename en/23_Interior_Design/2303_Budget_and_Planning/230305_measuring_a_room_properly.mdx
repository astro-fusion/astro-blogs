# Measuring a room properly for furniture and cupboards

Most furniture that does not fit was measured once, in the middle of a wall, with no thought for the door. After reading this you will be able to measure a room, check whether its corners are square, and write the numbers in a way your carpenter or a floor-plan tool can use.

## What you need

- A steel tape of at least 5 m (16 ft). A cloth tape sags and gives longer readings.
- A second person to hold the other end on long walls
- Paper, a pencil and a rubber
- A spirit level for checking walls and floors
- A phone for photos

Write everything in one unit. Centimetres are easiest, for example 360 cm for 3.6 m. Convert to feet and inches only at the end if you need to.

## First, a rough sketch

Draw the room as a box, even if it is not a box. Mark doors, windows, sockets, switches, the fan point, pillars and any wall that sticks out. Then add numbers to the sketch. A sketch with no scale is fine. Numbers matter, not drawing.

## The order to measure in

1. **Wall lengths.** Measure each wall at three heights: near the floor, at about waist height, and near the ceiling. Write all three. Use the smallest one for anything that must slide in between two walls, such as a wardrobe. Walls are often a few centimetres out because of plaster.
2. **Ceiling height.** Measure at each corner and the middle, from the floor to the ceiling. Use the smallest.
3. **Diagonals.** Measure corner to opposite corner, both ways. Equal diagonals mean a square room. See the worked example below.
4. **Openings.** Door: width and height of the clear opening, not of the frame, and how far it swings. Window: width, height, and height of the sill above the floor.
5. **Things that stick out.** Pillars, beams, a water pipe, a radiator, a meter box, the stair edge.
6. **Positions.** Measure from the nearest corner along the wall to each door, window, socket and switch. Always measure from the same corner, going the same direction, so the numbers add up.
7. **Floor level.** Place a spirit level on the floor and look. Note a slope near a bathroom or balcony door, which is often intentional for drainage.

## Worked example: a 3.0 x 3.6 m (10 x 12 ft) bedroom

You measure the walls at three heights:

| Wall | Floor | Middle | Ceiling | Use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Short wall A | 299 cm | 300 cm | 301 cm | 299 cm |
| Short wall B | 300 cm | 300 cm | 300 cm | 300 cm |
| Long wall C | 358 cm | 360 cm | 361 cm | 358 cm |
| Long wall D | 360 cm | 360 cm | 360 cm | 360 cm |

Floor area from the smallest figures: 299 x 358 = 107,042 sq cm, which is 10.7 sq m (115 sq ft). If you had used the nominal 300 x 360, you would get 10.8 sq m (116 sq ft). The difference is small for area but can be the difference between a wardrobe sliding in and not.

**Squareness check.** For a perfect 3.00 x 3.60 rectangle, each diagonal is the square root of 300 x 300 plus 360 x 360. That is 90,000 + 129,600 = 219,600, and its square root is about 468.6 cm (15 ft 4 in). You measure the two diagonals and get 468 cm and 471 cm. They differ by 3 cm, so the room is slightly out of square. For a loose bed that does not matter. For a built-in wardrobe or a fitted kitchen, tell your carpenter, so he scribes to the wall instead of assuming a right angle.

**Fit check for a bed.** A double bed is about 150 x 190 cm (4 ft 11 in x 6 ft 3 in). Put it against wall D (360 cm). Along wall D, the remaining length is 360 - 150 = 210 cm, which leaves room for a side table of 45 cm and a walking space of 75 cm (2 ft 6 in) at 45 + 75 = 120 cm, with 210 - 120 = 90 cm left over. The room works.

## Measure the route as well as the room

Before you buy a sofa, wardrobe or bed, measure the way it will travel: the lift, the stair width and its turns, the entrance door, the corridor and the bedroom door. Write the narrowest clear width and the lowest height. Look for stair railings, low beams and meter boxes. If an item will not turn the corner, ask if it can be delivered in parts.

## Write it down so someone else can use it

- Date and your name on the sketch
- One unit throughout, with the unit written
- Each dimension circled and labelled as wall A, wall B, and so on
- A photo of each wall with the tape visible
- Notes: "wall slants", "floor slopes to the left", "pipe at 20 cm from corner"

## Checklist

- Steel tape, second person, pencil, level, phone
- Three readings for every wall, use the smallest
- Ceiling height at five points
- Both diagonals compared
- Clear door and window openings, and door swing
- Positions of sockets and switches from one corner
- The delivery route measured
- Every number written in one unit

## Common mistakes

1. **Measuring once.** A single reading hides a leaning wall.
2. **Using the frame, not the clear opening.** The door frame is wider than the space you can carry through.
3. **Forgetting the skirting.** It takes a few centimetres at each wall base.
4. **Mixing units.** Feet and centimetres on one sheet lead to wrong math.
5. **Skipping the delivery route.** Many sofas have been returned from the stairwell.

## FAQ

### Why do I need the smallest reading?
Anything that slides between two walls must fit at the tightest point. The widest reading will not.

### What if the diagonals are very different?
A small difference of a few centimetres is normal. A larger one means a wall is out of square, and any built-in item must be cut to the real shape.

### Do I measure inside or outside the skirting?
Measure both. Use the inside figure for items that sit on the wall, and the figure above the skirting for items that stand away from the wall.

### How do I measure a room with a bay window or an angled wall?
Break it into rectangles and triangles, measure each and mark the angle with a tape from one point on each wall.

### Can I use a phone app instead of a tape?
A phone app is useful for a rough check. Use a tape for anything a carpenter will build.
