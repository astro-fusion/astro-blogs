# Working with a contractor: scope, stages and handover

A contractor job goes wrong when two people picture different results and nothing is written down. After reading this you will be able to write a plain scope, split payment into stages, handle extra work without a fight, and inspect the finish before you pay the last part.

## Write the scope first

The scope is a list of what will be done, in which room, with which material, to what finish. A good scope is boring and specific. Compare:

- Weak: "Renovate bathroom."
- Better: "Remove old floor and wall tiles. Re-lay the floor with a fall towards the drain. Tile walls to a height of 2.1 m (6 ft 11 in). Replace the tap, shower mixer and drain cover with the items listed on the sheet. Remove all debris from the site."

Add what is not included, for example "painting of the ceiling is not part of this job" or "waterproofing is done by a separate specialist." Anything about wiring, gas, waterproofing or structure should name the licensed person doing it. Do not let a general worker improvise those.

## Get at least two quotes and compare line by line

Give every contractor the same written scope. Put the quotes in columns, row by row. A much lower quote is usually missing something: cleanup, a coat of paint, the removal of debris, the material grade. Ask each one what is left out. Ask also who supervises on site each day, because the person who sells the job is often not the person who does it.

## Agree the stages and payments

Pay for work done, not for time passed. Split the job into stages, each with a visible finish, and attach a payment to each. Keep a part back until the end.

### Worked example: a bathroom job in 10 parts

Say the agreed price is cut into 10 equal parts, whatever the real amount is.

| Stage | When it counts as done | Parts |
|---|---|---|
| Advance | Agreement signed, materials ordered | 2 |
| Demolition and pipework | Old tiles out, new pipes in, pressure tested | 2 |
| Tiling | Floor and walls tiled, grout set | 3 |
| Fittings and finishing | Taps, shower, drain, door fitted | 2 |
| Retention | Snag list closed, site cleaned | 1 |

Check the arithmetic: 2 + 2 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 10 parts. At the end of tiling the contractor has been paid 2 + 2 + 3 = 7 of 10 parts, which is seven tenths. At handover he is owed the final 1 part, which is one tenth. That one tenth is your reason for a clean finish, because no one rushes off site when a payment is waiting.

If the contract also covers a second bathroom, repeat the table for it. Do not let the payments of one job pay for the other.

## Changes and extra work

Changes happen: you decide to move a tap, or a pipe turns out to be corroded. The rule is simple: no extra work without a written note, signed by both of you, stating what, why and how much. A text message that the contractor replies to also counts as written, but keep it. Add the extra amount to the next stage's payment so it is visible.

## Time

Ask for a start date, a finish date and what happens if work stops. Allow about a week more than the contractor says for a small job, and longer in the monsoon when plaster and paint dry slowly. Tell the contractor about festivals, exams and guests so the plan can avoid them. Check local rules about working hours in your building, and tell neighbours and the building committee before noisy work starts.

## Documents to keep

- The signed scope and quotation
- Payment record: date, amount, what it was for, who received it
- Any change notes
- Names and numbers of the contractor, the supervisor and any specialist (electrician, plumber)
- Photos of walls and floors before you start, and again before they are closed up

Photograph pipes and wiring inside walls before tiles or plaster hide them. You will thank yourself years later.

## Handover and the snag list

A snag list is a list of small faults to fix before the last payment. Do the walk with the contractor, in daylight, with a pencil and a torch:

- Tap every tile and listen for hollow sounds.
- Run all taps and flush all toilets. Look for leaks under sinks and at joints.
- Check a floor slope by pouring a bucket of water towards the drain and watching where it goes.
- Open and close every door, window, drawer and lock.
- Test every switch and socket by plugging in a charger or lamp. If something is not working, ask for the licensed electrician to check it.
- Look at paint and grout in side light for patches and gaps.

Write every item on the list, put a date next to each, and sign it. Pay the final part when the list is closed.

## Checklist before you sign

- Written scope with materials, finish and exclusions
- Two or more quotes compared on the same scope
- Stages with visible finishes and payment attached
- A part held back until the snag list is closed
- A change-note rule
- Start date, finish date and daily site contact
- Named licensed persons for electrical, gas and waterproofing work

## Common mistakes

1. **Paying a large advance.** A small advance covers materials. A large one removes your leverage.
2. **A scope that says only "renovate".** Every gap in the words becomes an argument later.
3. **Choosing only the lowest quote.** Check what is left out.
4. **Verbal changes.** Without a note nobody agrees later what was changed or why.
5. **Closing walls before photographing the pipes.** Photos are your map of what is inside.

## FAQ

### Should I use one contractor or hire each trade myself?
One contractor is simpler but you pay for the coordination. Hiring each trade saves money if you can schedule them yourself, but you carry the risk of gaps between them.

### What is retention?
A part of the price held back until the work is complete and the faults are fixed. In the example it is one tenth.

### Can the contractor ask for more money halfway?
Only for real changes that you approved in writing. If a hidden fault is found, ask for a written note stating the fault, the fix and the cost.

### Do I need a written agreement for a small job?
Yes. A one-page scope, a price and a payment plan is enough. Check local rules for anything bigger.

### Who is responsible for fixing faults after handover?
Agree this before you sign: for how long the contractor will return to fix faults, and who pays. Put it in writing.
