# Working with a carpenter: drawings, measurements and checks

Most carpentry disputes start with a conversation like "make it about this big". After reading this you will be able to give your carpenter a dimensioned sketch, agree the materials in writing, and check the work at each stage before you pay.

## Start with a sketch, not a description

You do not need to draw well. You need numbers. On plain paper, draw the wall as a rectangle and write:

- the wall width and the ceiling height, measured at three places each, using the smallest number (see the article on measuring a room properly)
- the depth you want: about 55 to 60 cm (1 ft 10 in to 2 ft) for a wardrobe, about 35 to 45 cm (1 ft 2 in to 1 ft 6 in) for a bookshelf
- where doors open, where sockets, switches and windows are, and which way the skirting runs
- the plinth, which is the solid base the cupboard stands on, usually about 7 to 10 cm (3 to 4 in)

Take a photo of the wall with a tape measure in it. The carpenter will look at it again when he is at the workshop and you are not there.

## Agree materials and fittings in writing

Write on the sketch, or on a separate sheet, what the carpenter will use:

- **Board:** the type and thickness. Common carcass thickness is 18 mm (about 3/4 in). The back panel is usually thinner.
- **Sheet size:** plywood and boards are commonly sold as 2440 x 1220 mm (8 x 4 ft). Planning the cuts around this size reduces waste.
- **Finish:** laminate, veneer or paint, and which edges are taped.
- **Hinges and drawer runners:** the type and number. Heavy doors need more hinges.
- **Handles, locks and shelves:** how many and where.

Ask the carpenter to sign or write his name next to the list and keep a photo of it. This is not about distrust. It stops later arguments.

## Worked example: a three-door wardrobe on a 2.4 m wall

Take a bedroom wall that measures 240 cm (7 ft 10 in) wide. The wall is slightly uneven, so you leave 2 cm on each side for scribing, which is trimming to the wall's shape.

**Outer width:** 240 - 2 - 2 = 236 cm.

**Panels across the width:** two sides and two dividers make four vertical panels. At 18 mm (1.8 cm) each, they use 4 x 1.8 = 7.2 cm.

**Clear width inside:** 236 - 7.2 = 228.8 cm.

**Width of each of three bays:** 228.8 / 3 = about 76.3 cm (2 ft 6 in). That is a comfortable hanging width for shirts and kurtas on hangers.

**Door width:** the outer width 236 cm divided by 3 is about 78.7 cm per door. Ask the carpenter to leave a gap of a few millimetres between doors so they do not rub.

**Board count:** the ceiling is 270 cm (8 ft 10 in) and the wardrobe is 240 cm (7 ft 10 in) high. A vertical panel is 240 cm high and 55 cm deep. A sheet is 244 cm long and 122 cm wide. Two panels side by side use 55 + 55 = 110 cm of the 122 cm width, and 240 cm fits the 244 cm length. So one sheet gives two vertical panels. Four panels need 4 / 2 = 2 sheets. The shelves, base, top and doors need more sheets, and the carpenter will count them. The point is that you can check his count.

## Checks while the work is going on

- **Before cutting:** ask him to check the sketch numbers on the wall himself. Two measurers are better than one.
- **After the carcass is up, before doors:** use a spirit level on the top and the sides. Open the empty carcass and look at the corners. Gaps wider than a thumbnail are a warning.
- **After doors:** open and close each door ten times. Look for rubbing, loose hinges and uneven gaps.
- **After fittings:** pull each drawer out fully and push it back. Load a shelf with books and see if it bends.
- **Finish:** run your hand along the edges. Edges should be sealed, not rough.

## Payment

Pay in steps linked to the work: a small advance for materials, a part when the carcass is up, and the last part when everything works and the site is clean. Hold back a small part until you have lived with the furniture for a week. Put the amounts in writing. Keep receipts for the board if you buy it yourself.

## Checklist for the carpenter visit

- A sketch with wall width, height and depth, and the position of sockets and windows
- The smallest of three measurements used for each dimension
- Board type, thickness, finish, hinge and runner details written down
- A date for the visit, a date for delivery and the payment steps
- A spirit level, a tape measure and a torch in your hand on the day

## Common mistakes

1. **Measuring once.** Walls are rarely straight. Measure at the top, middle and bottom.
2. **Forgetting the plinth and skirting.** A cupboard that must sit tight to the wall needs to clear the skirting.
3. **Not writing the material.** "Good plywood" means different things to different people.
4. **Paying the full amount early.** You lose the reason for the carpenter to finish tidily.
5. **No allowance for damp.** In monsoon-prone homes, ask for sealed edges and keep the back of a wardrobe a little away from an outer wall.

## FAQ

### Should I buy the board myself?
Either works. If the carpenter buys it, the quote should say the type and thickness. If you buy it, ask him for the sheet count first.

### How deep should a wardrobe be?
About 55 to 60 cm (1 ft 10 in to 2 ft) so hangers fit with the clothes facing sideways. Less than that and clothes crush against the door.

### Is a sliding door better than a hinged door?
Sliding doors save the swing space in a narrow room but only half of the wardrobe is open at once. Hinged doors open the whole bay but need clear floor in front.

### How long does a wardrobe take?
Allow about two weeks for a standard built-in wardrobe once the materials are in, and longer in the monsoon when finishes dry slowly.

### What if the work is not what we agreed?
Point to the written list and the sketch, stop payment for that stage, and agree a fix with a date. Keep it in writing.
