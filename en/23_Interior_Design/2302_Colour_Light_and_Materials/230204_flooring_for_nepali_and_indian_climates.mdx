# Flooring for Nepali and Indian Climates: What Suits Where

The floor is the largest surface in a room, and the hardest to change later. After reading this you will know how common flooring types behave in a hot plains summer, a cold hill winter and a wet monsoon, and how to count tiles for a real room.

## What the floor has to cope with

- **Heat.** Stone and tile stay cooler under bare feet in a Terai or plains summer.
- **Cold.** Stone and tile also feel colder in a hill winter. Wood, cork and rugs feel warmer to the touch.
- **Water and damp.** Monsoon brings wet shoes, humid air and, on ground floors, moisture rising from below.
- **Shoes off at the door.** In many homes the floor takes bare feet and children playing on it.
- **Cleaning.** Dust and mopping are daily jobs.

## The common options

| Flooring | Strengths | Watch out for | Cost level |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vitrified or porcelain tile | Hard, easy to clean, many sizes | Cold in winter, slippery when wet if glossy | Medium |
| Ceramic tile | Cheaper, good for walls and light-use floors | Less hard-wearing, can chip | Low to medium |
| Marble | Cool, long life | Stains, needs polishing, can be slippery | High |
| Granite | Very hard, good for kitchens and entrances | Heavy, cold, limited colours | Medium to high |
| Kota or other natural stone | Rough surface gives grip, stays cool | Needs sealing, can mark | Low to medium |
| Wood | Warm underfoot | Swells with damp, needs care in monsoon | High |
| Laminate | Warm look, quick to fit | Edges swell if water gets in | Medium |
| Vinyl plank | Water-resistant, warmer than tile | Can dent under heavy legs | Low to medium |

Costs are relative and change by region and quality.

## Choosing by climate

**Hot plains and Terai.** Tile and stone are comfortable. Light colours reflect more heat into the room than dark floors in direct sun. Avoid very glossy finishes near entrances, because they are slippery when feet are wet.

**Hill homes with cold winters.** Use wood, laminate or vinyl in bedrooms, and put a rug of about 1.5 x 2.1 m (4 ft 11 in x 6 ft 11 in) beside the bed. Tile is fine in kitchens and bathrooms. Heating, stoves and chimneys are for a qualified person to fit.

**Monsoon and ground floors.** Be careful with wood and laminate. Damp from the slab can lift them. Ask the contractor about a damp-proof layer under the floor and about waterproofing; this is a professional job and not something to improvise. Tile or stone is safer where damp is a risk.

**Bathrooms and balconies.** Choose tiles with a textured or matt surface that grips when wet. Ask the shop for the slip rating. A balcony floor is outdoors, so the tile should be rated for it, and any change to slab or drainage needs a licensed engineer.

## Size of tile

Common sizes are 600 x 600 mm (2 ft x 2 ft), 600 x 1200 mm (2 ft x 4 ft) and 800 x 800 mm (2 ft 7 in x 2 ft 7 in). Larger tiles mean fewer joints and easier cleaning, but they need a flat base and are harder to carry up stairs. In a small room, 600 x 600 mm is easy to lay and cut.

Joints are usually 2 to 3 mm (about one-tenth of an inch). The base must be level before tiling. If the slab is uneven, the contractor levels it first.

## Worked example: tiling a 3.0 x 3.6 m bedroom

The room is 3.0 x 3.6 m (9 ft 10 in x 11 ft 10 in). You choose a matt vitrified tile of 600 x 600 mm (2 ft x 2 ft).

**Step 1: floor area.** 3.0 x 3.6 = 10.8 m² (about 116 sq ft).

**Step 2: area of one tile.** 0.6 x 0.6 = 0.36 m² (about 3.9 sq ft).

**Step 3: tiles needed.** 10.8 divided by 0.36 = 30 tiles. Check with rows: 3.0 divided by 0.6 = 5 tiles across, and 3.6 divided by 0.6 = 6 tiles along, so 5 x 6 = 30.

**Step 4: extra for cuts and breakage.** Allow about one extra tile for every ten, so 3 more tiles. Total 33.

**Step 5: boxes.** Tiles often come 4 to a box, so 33 divided by 4 = 8.25. Buy 9 boxes, which is 36 tiles. Keep the 3 spare tiles for repairs, and note the batch number so later tiles match.

**Compare with 800 x 800 mm.** Each tile is 0.64 m² (about 6.9 sq ft). Along 3.0 m you fit 3.75 tiles, and along 3.6 m you fit 4.5, so you need 4 x 5 = 20 tiles and many cuts. That is 20 x 0.64 = 12.8 m² (about 138 sq ft) of tile for a 10.8 m² (about 116 sq ft) room, or 2.0 m² (about 22 sq ft) wasted. The smaller tile fits this room better.

Check measurements on the actual floor, not only the drawing, because walls are seldom perfectly square.

## A short checklist

- Note whether the room is on the ground floor, a middle floor or the top floor.
- Check for damp patches or stained skirting.
- Choose a finish with grip for the entrance, bathroom and balcony.
- Measure the room twice.
- Divide the floor area by the area of one tile and add spares.
- Buy all tiles from one batch.
- Ask the contractor how the base will be levelled and cured.
- Keep leftover tiles and the batch number.

## Common mistakes

- **Glossy tiles at the entrance or bathroom.** They are slippery when wet.
- **Wood on a damp ground floor.** It swells and lifts.
- **Buying the exact count.** There is no spare for cuts, breakage or future repairs.
- **Mixing batches.** Colour and size can differ slightly.
- **Tiling over an uneven or damp base.** The tiles crack or lift.

## FAQ

### Which flooring is coolest in summer?

Stone and tile stay cooler than wood or laminate. Light colours reflect more heat.

### Can I put wooden flooring in a ground floor flat?

Only after checking for damp and asking the contractor about a damp-proof layer. Tile is safer in monsoon-prone ground floors.

### How do I choose a non-slip tile?

Choose a matt or textured finish and ask the shop for the slip rating for wet areas.

### How many extra tiles should I order?

About one extra for every ten is a common allowance. Cut-heavy layouts and diagonal laying need more.

### Is laminate waterproof?

Most laminate is not. Water that gets in at the edges makes it swell. Vinyl plank copes better with water.

### Can I lay new tiles over old ones?

Sometimes, but the height changes and the old base must be sound. Ask your contractor, and check door clearances.
