# Colour in a Small Room: What Really Helps

Paint advice for small rooms is often "go white". The truth is more useful. After reading this you will know what colour does to a room physically, how to test a shade at home and how to split colours across the walls of a small bedroom.

## What colour does to a room

Colour changes how much light a surface sends back. That is all it does physically, and it is enough to matter.

- **Light colours reflect more light.** A pale wall sends daylight and lamp light back into the room. The room needs less lamp light and shadows are softer.
- **Dark colours absorb light.** The room can look smaller at the edges and needs brighter lamps. A deep colour can still look rich, but it works best on one wall.
- **Colour is also affected by what is next to it.** A cream next to a white looks yellow. The same cream next to a strong yellow looks grey.

Paint makers sometimes give a **light reflectance value (LRV)** from 0 for black to 100 for white. A higher value means a lighter colour. Ask the dealer if it is printed on the shade card. If it is not, judge by eye in your own light.

## The light in your room decides the shade

Look at the room at three times of day: morning, noon and evening.

- **North-facing room:** the daylight is cool and soft. Colours with a slight warm base keep it from looking grey.
- **South or west-facing room:** the light is strong and warm. Very warm colours can look yellow or orange. A neutral or slightly cool pale colour keeps it calm.
- **Rooms with little daylight, such as inner rooms or lower floors:** choose pale colours and give them a matt finish that does not shine glare back.
- **At night:** lamp colour changes the paint. A warm lamp deepens yellows and reds. Test paint under the lamps you will use.

## Test before you buy

1. Buy a small sample pot.
2. Paint two patches of about 60 x 60 cm (2 x 2 ft) on two different walls, preferably one near the window and one in a corner.
3. Look at them in the morning, at noon and in the evening under the lamps.
4. Wait for the paint to dry fully, because wet paint looks darker.
5. Hold furniture, curtains and the floor sample next to it.

## Ways to place colour

- **One light colour throughout.** The simplest choice for a small room. Walls and ceiling in close shades make the room look continuous.
- **Light walls, a deeper tone on one wall.** Choose the wall you see first, usually behind the bed or the sofa. Keep the deeper tone away from the window wall, which is already in strong light.
- **Same colour for walls and skirting.** Hides the line where the wall ends.
- **Colour in things, not walls.** Cushions, a rug and curtains can bring colour into a room and are cheap to swap.

## Worked example: a 2.7 x 3.0 m bedroom

Take a bedroom 2.7 x 3.0 m (about 9 x 10 ft), ceiling 2.7 m. The bed head is on a 2.7 m wall with no openings. The door is 0.9 x 2.1 m and the window 1.2 x 1.2 m, both on the other walls.

**Step 1: gross wall area.** The perimeter is 2 x (2.7 + 3.0) = 11.4 m. 11.4 x 2.7 = 30.78 square metres.

**Step 2: openings.** Door: 0.9 x 2.1 = 1.89. Window: 1.2 x 1.2 = 1.44. Together 3.33 square metres.

**Step 3: net paintable wall area.** 30.78 minus 3.33 = 27.45 square metres.

**Step 4: the accent wall.** The bed wall is 2.7 x 2.7 = 7.29 square metres.

**Step 5: the split.** 7.29 / 27.45 = 0.27, which is a little over a quarter of the wall area. The other three walls and the ceiling stay in the light colour: 27.45 minus 7.29 = 20.16 square metres of walls, plus the ceiling 2.7 x 3.0 = 8.1 square metres.

**Step 6: what this gives you.** A deeper tone covers about a quarter of the wall paint, which is enough to see and not enough to close the room in. Buy paint for each colour separately and read the coverage on the tin, since it differs by brand and surface.

## Checklist

- Look at the room at three times of day.
- Note which way the main window faces.
- Choose a pale base for walls and ceiling.
- Decide if one wall gets a deeper tone, and which one.
- Paint test patches on two walls and check them under your lamps.
- Hold curtain, bed linen and floor samples next to them.
- Buy all paint of one shade in one go, from the same batch.

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing from a small card.** A card is far smaller than a wall, so the colour looks stronger on the wall than on the card.
- **Skipping the test patch.** Daylight and lamp light change a shade.
- **A deep colour on all four walls of a tiny room.** The room loses its edges and needs more lamp light.
- **Glossy finish on a pale wall.** It shows every ripple and throws glare.
- **Painting over damp.** Peeling and stains return. Fix the leak or the seepage first, and call a professional if the wall stays damp.

## FAQ

### Is white always best for a small room?
Not always. A pale warm or pale cool shade can look better than a flat white and reflects nearly as much light.

### Does a dark colour make a room look smaller?
It can make the edges less visible. Use it on one wall and keep the others light.

### What colour for a north-facing room?
A light colour with a slight warm base, tested in the room first.

### Should the ceiling be white?
A pale matt ceiling is a safe choice. Match it to the wall tone if you want a continuous look.

### How do I stop the wall colour looking different at night?
Test the sample under the lamps you plan to use, and choose bulbs of the same warmth in the whole room.
