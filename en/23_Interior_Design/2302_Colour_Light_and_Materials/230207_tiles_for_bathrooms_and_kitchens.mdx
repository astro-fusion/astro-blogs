# Tiles for Bathrooms and Kitchens: Size, Grip and Grout

Tiles in wet rooms have to be easy to clean, hard to slip on and set properly. After reading this you will be able to pick a size and surface, ask the tiler the right questions and count how many tiles a small bathroom needs.

## Floor tiles and wall tiles are different

A wall tile only has to look good and wipe clean. A floor tile also has to grip a wet foot. Do not use a wall tile on a floor, even if you like the pattern.

For bathroom and kitchen floors, ask for a **matt or textured surface**. Tile makers publish a slip rating, often written R9 to R13, where a higher number grips more. A bathroom floor is commonly given R10 or R11, and a shower floor R11 or above. Ask the dealer for the rating, and test the sample by wetting it and rubbing your bare foot across it.

## Size

- **Small bathroom floor:** 300 x 300 mm (1 ft x 1 ft) tiles are easy to slope toward a drain, and the many joints give extra grip.
- **Larger tiles on a floor:** 600 x 600 mm tiles have fewer joints, but they do not follow a slope well in a small shower area. Use them only on the flat dry zone.
- **Wall tiles:** 300 x 450 or 300 x 600 mm. Taller tiles mean fewer horizontal lines.
- **Kitchen floor:** 600 x 600 mm in a matt or lightly textured finish works for a larger kitchen. A very glossy floor is slippery with oil and water.

## Grout and joints

Grout is the filler between tiles. It decides how the floor ages.

- Ask for a joint of 2 to 3 mm on walls and 3 to 5 mm on floors.
- A thin joint looks neat but gives less room for the tile to move and for the tiler to correct small errors.
- Use a stain-resistant grout for kitchens and wet bathrooms. Light grout stains with turmeric and oil, and dark grout hides this better.
- Epoxy grout is harder to stain but is harder to apply. Ask whether your tiler has used it before.

## The slope and the waterproofing

A bathroom floor must fall toward the drain so water runs off. A gentle fall of about 10 mm per metre (1 in 100) is commonly used. Waterproofing sits under the tiles. It is not a job to guess at. Ask the contractor what product and how many coats, and ask for a water test over a day or two, in which the floor is flooded and the ceiling below is checked. A licensed professional should do the waterproofing.

## Kitchen walls

- The counter is usually 85 to 90 cm (2 ft 9 in to 2 ft 11 in) high.
- The space between the counter and the wall units is commonly about 60 cm (2 ft). Tile this strip to the underside of the wall units.
- Behind the hob, use a surface that handles heat and wipes clean of oil. Ask the dealer for a tile or a panel suited to cooking heat.
- Extend the tile at least 60 cm above the sink if the wall gets wet.

## Worked example: a 1.5 x 2.1 m bathroom

Take a bathroom 1.5 x 2.1 m (about 5 x 7 ft) with a ceiling height of 2.1 m of tiling. It has a door 0.75 m wide and a window 0.6 x 0.6 m. Floor tiles are 300 x 300 mm, wall tiles 300 x 600 mm.

**Step 1: floor area.** 1.5 x 2.1 = 3.15 square metres.

**Step 2: floor tiles.** One tile is 0.3 x 0.3 = 0.09 square metres. 3.15 / 0.09 = 35 tiles. Cuts and breakage need extras, so add about one tile in ten: 35 plus 4 gives 39 tiles. Round up to full boxes.

**Step 3: wall perimeter.** 2 x (1.5 + 2.1) = 7.2 m. Subtract the door width: 7.2 minus 0.75 = 6.45 m of wall.

**Step 4: wall area.** 6.45 x 2.1 = 13.545 square metres. Subtract the window: 0.6 x 0.6 = 0.36. 13.545 minus 0.36 = 13.185 square metres.

**Step 5: wall tiles.** One tile is 0.3 x 0.6 = 0.18 square metres. 13.185 / 0.18 = 73.25, so 74 tiles. Add about one in ten: 74 plus 7 or 8 gives about 82 tiles.

**Step 6: check the tile height.** Tiles 0.6 m tall fit 2.1 m in exactly 3.5 rows, so one row must be cut. Plan where the cut row goes, for example at the top, where it is least visible. Or choose a tiling height of 2.4 m, which is four full rows.

Always buy all tiles from one batch. Shades differ between batches, and you cannot match them later.

## Checklist

- Hold the floor sample wet and test it with a bare foot.
- Ask the dealer for the slip rating of the floor tile.
- Agree the joint width and grout type in writing.
- Confirm the plan for waterproofing and the water test.
- Mark the drain and check the floor has a fall toward it.
- Count tiles with extras and buy from one batch.
- Plan the cut row so it does not land at the door or at eye level.

## Common mistakes

- **Glossy floor tiles in a wet room.** They look good in the shop and slip at home.
- **Large tiles on the shower floor.** They cannot follow the slope, so water pools.
- **Tiling before the water test.** A leak found after tiling means breaking the work.
- **Not matching the drain level.** Tiles that finish above the drain trap water.
- **Forgetting the damp season.** Choose a surface that dries in humid weather, and keep a window or exhaust fan working.

## FAQ

### Should bathroom floor tiles be matt or glossy?
Matt or textured. Gloss becomes slippery with soap and water.

### What size tile is best for a small bathroom?
Floor tiles of 300 x 300 mm and wall tiles of 300 x 600 mm are a common, easy-to-work choice.

### How wide should grout lines be?
About 2 to 3 mm on walls and 3 to 5 mm on floors, as a rule of thumb.

### How many extra tiles should I buy?
About one in ten. Buy more if the tile is laid on a diagonal or has a strong pattern.

### Can I tile over old tiles?
Sometimes, if they are firmly stuck and level. Ask the contractor, and note it raises the floor and may affect the door.
