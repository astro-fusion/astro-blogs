# Paint Finishes and Where to Use Each in Your Home

Colour is half of a paint choice. The other half is the finish, which decides how shiny the wall is, how easily it wipes clean and how many flaws it shows. After reading this you will choose a finish for each room and count the paint for a kitchen.

## What a finish is

Finish describes how much light bounces off the dried paint. A flat finish scatters light and looks soft. A glossy finish reflects light sharply and looks shiny. The glossier the paint, the more it shows every bump and patch on the wall, and the easier it is to wipe.

| Finish | Look | Wipes clean | Hides wall flaws |
|---|---|---|---|
| Matt or flat | No shine, soft | Poorly to fairly | Best |
| Low sheen or eggshell | Faint shine | Fairly well | Well |
| Satin | Soft shine | Well | Fairly |
| Semi-gloss | Clear shine | Very well | Poorly |
| Gloss | Mirror-like | Very well | Worst |

Washability varies a lot by product. Read the tin for scrub resistance, and do not assume a finish name guarantees it.

## Wall type and wall flaws

Fresh plaster that is not perfectly smooth looks best in matt. A side window throws light along the wall at a low angle, which makes bumps and trowel marks stand out, especially in a shiny finish. If your walls are old or uneven, choose a flatter finish, or fill and sand the wall before painting.

New walls need to dry out before paint goes on. Ask the painter or contractor how long to wait after plastering. Patches of damp, peeling or white powder mean there is a cause, such as a leak or rising damp, that paint will not fix. See a professional about the leak first.

## Where to use each finish

- **Ceilings.** Matt. It hides ripples and reduces glare from lamps. A white or very pale ceiling helps spread light.
- **Living room and bedroom walls.** Matt or low sheen. They look soft, and flaws show less.
- **Children's rooms and corridors.** Low sheen or satin. Hands and school bags leave marks, and a wipeable surface helps.
- **Kitchen walls.** Satin or washable low sheen. Steam and cooking splashes are easier to wipe. Behind the stove, tile is better than paint.
- **Bathrooms and utility rooms.** Satin or semi-gloss paint made for damp rooms, on walls above the tiles. Good ventilation matters more than the paint, so keep a window open or run the exhaust fan.
- **Doors, window frames and skirting.** Enamel or semi-gloss, on wood or metal. It takes knocks and cleaning.
- **Exterior walls and balconies.** Use exterior paint made for sun and rain. Anything involving scaffolding or work at height is for the contractor.
- **Puja shelf or niche.** A matt finish is fine. Keep the area behind a lamp or flame free of flammable cloth.

## Sheen and light

Under bright, direct light a glossy wall can mirror the lamp. In a room with a large window or downlights, choose a flatter finish on the wall that faces the light.

## Worked example: a 2.4 x 3.0 m kitchen

The kitchen is 2.4 x 3.0 m (7 ft 10 in x 9 ft 10 in) with a ceiling height of 2.8 m (9 ft 2 in). It has one door of 0.9 x 2.1 m (3 x 7 ft) and one window of 0.9 x 1.2 m (3 x 4 ft). A tile backsplash 0.6 m (2 ft) high runs along the 3.0 m (9 ft 10 in) counter wall. You choose satin for the walls and matt for the ceiling.

**Step 1: walls.**

- Distance around the room: 2 x (2.4 + 3.0) = 10.8 m (35 ft 5 in).
- Wall area: 10.8 x 2.8 = 30.24 m² (about 325 sq ft).
- Backsplash: 3.0 x 0.6 = 1.8 m² (about 19 sq ft).
- Door: 0.9 x 2.1 = 1.89 m² (about 20 sq ft).
- Window: 0.9 x 1.2 = 1.08 m² (about 12 sq ft).
- Paintable wall: 30.24 - 1.8 - 1.89 - 1.08 = 25.47 m² (about 274 sq ft).

**Step 2: litres of satin.** Assume about 10 m² per litre per coat (about 108 sq ft). Check your tin. Two coats: 25.47 x 2 = 50.94 m² (about 548 sq ft). Divide by 10: 5.1 litres. Buy 5 litres plus a 1 litre tin.

**Step 3: ceiling.** Floor area is 2.4 x 3.0 = 7.2 m² (about 77 sq ft). Two coats: 14.4 m² (about 155 sq ft). That is about 1.4 litres of matt, so buy 2 litres.

**Step 4: enamel for the door.** Both sides: 2 x 1.89 = 3.78 m² (about 41 sq ft). Add the frame and architrave by eye. A small tin of 1 litre is enough.

## A short checklist

- Look at the wall under the side light for bumps and patches.
- Fix leaks, cracks and damp before painting.
- Pick matt for ceilings and most living walls.
- Pick low sheen or satin for kitchens, corridors and children's rooms.
- Pick enamel for doors and frames.
- Measure, subtract openings, multiply by coats and divide by the coverage on the tin.
- Test the finish on a small patch under your own lamps.

## Common mistakes

- **Gloss on uneven walls.** Every flaw shines.
- **Matt in a wet kitchen.** It stains and is hard to wipe.
- **Painting over damp.** The paint blisters, and the cause remains.
- **Ignoring primer.** Bare or patched walls need a primer coat. Ask the shop which one suits your paint.
- **Buying the exact litres.** Leave a small margin, and keep a tin for touch-ups.

## FAQ

### Is matt or satin better for living rooms?

Matt looks softest and hides flaws. Satin is easier to wipe. Choose matt or low sheen if you do not have small children.

### What paint should I use on the bathroom ceiling?

Use a paint made for damp rooms, and make sure the room has ventilation. If mould keeps returning, ask a professional to check for a leak.

### Can I use enamel on walls?

You can, but its shine shows every flaw. It suits doors, grills and trims better.

### Does a shiny finish make a room look brighter?

It reflects light, but it also creates glare and shows flaws. Colour has a larger effect on brightness than finish.

### How long should I wait between coats?

Follow the time printed on the tin. Warm dry weather speeds drying, and humid monsoon days slow it.

### Should I paint during the monsoon?

Damp walls and humid air make drying slower and can spoil the finish. If you have to, wait for a dry spell and let the wall dry fully.
