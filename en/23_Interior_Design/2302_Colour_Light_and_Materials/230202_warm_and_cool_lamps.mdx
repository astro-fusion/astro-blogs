# Warm and Cool Lamps: Which Light for Which Room

Walk into a bulb shop and the boxes say things like 2700 K, 4000 K and 800 lm. This article explains what those numbers mean, so you can choose the right lamp for each room and work out how bright a room needs to be.

## Two numbers on every LED box

**Kelvin (K)** tells you the warmth of the light. A low number is warm and yellow. A high number is cool and bluish.

**Lumens (lm)** tell you how much light the lamp gives out. Watts only tell you how much power it uses, and LEDs give very different light for the same watts. Compare lumens, not watts.

A third number worth looking for is the colour rendering index, written CRI or Ra. A value of 80 or more means colours look reasonably true. A low value makes skin, food and fabric look dull or off.

## What the kelvin numbers look like

| Rating | Common name | How it looks |
|---|---|---|
| About 2700 K | Warm white | Yellowish, like an old filament bulb |
| About 3000 K | Soft white | Slightly less yellow, still warm |
| About 4000 K | Neutral white | Clean and fairly neutral |
| About 5000 to 6500 K | Cool white or daylight | White to bluish, crisp, can feel harsh at night |

These names vary by maker, so read the K figure on the box and not the label.

## Choosing by room

- **Living room.** Warm white, 2700 to 3000 K. Furniture, wood and skin tones look rich in warm light, and the room feels softer in the evening.
- **Bedroom.** Warm white, 2700 to 3000 K, for the main light and bedside lamps. A cool light in a bedroom is bright and flat, and it makes the wall colour look colder.
- **Kitchen.** Neutral white, about 4000 K, for the ceiling and under-cabinet lights. You want to see the colour of food and the edge of the knife clearly.
- **Study corner.** Neutral white, 4000 K, on the desk, with a lamp that does not shine into the screen or the page.
- **Bathroom.** Neutral white around the mirror so faces look true, and warm white elsewhere if you prefer.
- **Corridor and stairs.** Neutral or warm, with enough lumens that steps are easy to see.
- **Puja space.** Warm white suits a lamp-lit corner. Keep a diya or flame away from curtains and hanging cloth.

## Do not mix tones in one room

If the ceiling lights are 6500 K and a floor lamp is 2700 K, the room looks patchy. Wall colour looks different near each lamp. Pick one family of temperature for the room, and vary the brightness instead.

When you replace bulbs one at a time, write the K number on the inside of the switch plate cover or in your phone. You will not remember it a year later.

## How bright should a room be

There is no single correct brightness. A common practical guide is to aim for about 100 to 150 lumens per m² (about 9 to 14 lumens per sq ft) for a bedroom or living room's general light, and more, about 200 to 300 lumens per m² (about 19 to 28 lumens per sq ft), for kitchens and work areas. Treat these as starting points. Pale walls need less than dark walls, and older eyes usually want more.

Many Nepali and Indian homes have power cuts. A few bulbs on an inverter circuit, or a battery lamp near the stairs, are practical. Wiring and the inverter circuit are a job for a licensed electrician.

## Worked example: a bedroom

Take a 3.0 x 3.6 m (9 ft 10 in x 11 ft 10 in) bedroom. You want warm light at about 120 lumens per m².

- Floor area: 3.0 x 3.6 = 10.8 m² (about 116 sq ft).
- Target total light: 10.8 x 120 = 1,296 lumens.
- Choose lamps from what is sold in 2700 K. Suppose one ceiling light gives 600 lumens, and two bedside lamps give 350 lumens each.
- Add them up: 600 + 350 + 350 = 1,300 lumens, which matches the target.

If the room has a dark wardrobe wall and dark floor, move up to about 150 lumens per m². That gives 10.8 x 150 = 1,620 lumens, so add a 320-lumen wall lamp or replace the ceiling light with a brighter one.

For a 2.4 x 3.0 m (7 ft 10 in x 9 ft 10 in) kitchen at 250 lumens per m², the floor area is 7.2 m² (about 77 sq ft), so the target is 7.2 x 250 = 1,800 lumens, in 4000 K lamps. Put some of it under the wall cabinets so the counter is not in your own shadow.

## A short checklist

- Read the K number and the lumens on the box.
- Choose one K family per room.
- Look for CRI 80 or more.
- Measure floor area and multiply by the lumens per m² you want.
- Add up the lumens of every lamp that will be on.
- Put the brightest light where you work, cook or read.
- Ask an electrician for anything that needs wiring or a dimmer.

## Common mistakes

- **Buying by watts.** Two 9 W bulbs can give very different brightness.
- **Putting cool daylight bulbs in a bedroom.** The light is bright and the room looks colder in the evening.
- **Mixing temperatures in one room.** The walls look uneven in colour.
- **One central bulb for everything.** You get shadow where you work and a bright glare when you look up.
- **Choosing a bulb by the shop's lighting.** Take the box home and check one lamp in the room first.

## FAQ

### Is warm white or cool white better for a home?

Neither is better everywhere. Warm white suits living areas and bedrooms. Neutral white suits kitchens, study desks and bathroom mirrors.

### What does 800 lumens mean in practice?

It is a measure of how much light the lamp gives out. A single ceiling lamp of 800 lumens fills a small room with moderate light. Add the lumens of all the lamps to judge the whole room.

### Can I use daylight bulbs everywhere?

You can, but the light is crisp and bluish, and it can feel harsh in the evening. Many people keep it for work areas only.

### Do LED bulbs have to match the colour of the old bulb?

No. Choose the K number you want. Check one lamp in the room before you buy a full set.

### Does a dimmer change the colour?

Some LEDs stay at the same K when dimmed, and some go slightly warmer. The box usually states whether the lamp is dimmable, and the dimmer itself is installed by an electrician.

### How many lamps should a room have?

Several small light sources are more comfortable than one strong light. A ceiling light plus two or three lamps at lower height gives you choices.
